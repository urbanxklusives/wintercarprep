Driving in Snow: What to Do When Your Car Starts Sliding

A slide in snow lasts about three seconds. What your hands and feet do in those three seconds decides everything.

Advertisement

You're driving through fresh snow, the back end steps out, and suddenly the car is pointing somewhere you didn't choose. Your instincts scream two things: slam the brakes, and yank the steering wheel. Both make it worse. A slide feels like chaos, but it's physics — and physics has a right answer. Knowing it before it happens is the difference between a scare and a crash.

Why it's wrong

Slamming the brakes in a slide locks the wheels, and locked wheels don't steer — they just slide in whatever direction the car is already going. Yanking the steering wheel the other way overcorrects, so the car snaps back past straight and slides the other direction, often harder. And stomping the gas makes the drive wheels spin faster, which means less grip, not more. The worst slides aren't caused by the snow — they're caused by the driver's reaction to the snow. Every panicked input takes grip away from tires that are already at their limit.

The right way

  1. Ease off the gas — don't slam the brakes. Lifting off the throttle transfers weight forward and lets the tires regain grip. If you have anti-lock brakes (ABS), a firm steady press is fine — the system pulses the brakes for you. Don't pump ABS brakes; that's an old habit from before ABS existed.
  2. Look where you want to go, not at what you're afraid of hitting. Your hands follow your eyes. Stare at the ditch and you'll steer into it. Look down the road where you want the car to be.
  3. Steer gently in the direction of the slide. If the rear end slides left, steer left — small, smooth corrections, not big swings. The goal is to point the front wheels in the direction the car is actually travelling until grip returns.
  4. Wait for grip, then straighten out. The moment the tires bite again, gently bring the wheel back to centre. The whole thing takes a few seconds.
  5. Slow down before the next corner, not in it. The real secret to winter driving is doing all your braking and slowing on the straight, then rolling through corners gently. Smooth inputs, no surprises.
  6. Leave double the following distance. Stopping distances on snow can be several times longer than on dry pavement. The gap in front of you is your safety margin — protect it.

The rundown

Off the gas, eyes where you want to go, gentle steering into the slide, straighten out when grip returns. No brake slamming, no yanking, no stomping. And the best slide is the one that never happens: slow down early, keep your distance, and make every input smooth. Winter driving rewards patience more than skill.
